§ 2 — ConstructionÀ lire avec la table traçante de l'atelier — déplacez t là-bas et regardez l'échelle se réduire

Construction — de Casteljau

How the point is found, without any formula

  1. Join the pointsDraw the control polygon P₀–P₁–P₂–P₃. It is a rough sketch of the curve you want.
  2. Divide every segment by tOn each side, mark the point a fraction t of the way along. Join those marks — a smaller polygon appears.
  3. Repeat until one point remainsDivide again, and again. The last point sits exactly on the curve. Do it for every t from 0 to 1 and the pen draws the whole curve.

Paul de Casteljau found this construction at Citroën in 1959. Kept as a trade secret, it could not be published — so the curve carries Bézier's name.

Lecture de l'échelle

What you see on the plotter

On the Atelier tab, switch Construction on and drag the t fader. The coloured segments are this construction, live:

Level 1 — one point on each side of the polygon, each a fraction t along its side.
Level 2 — the same division applied to the smaller polygon those points make.
Final point — B(t) — after enough rounds only one point remains, and it lies exactly on the curve.

No formula is evaluated to find it — only repeated straight-line division. That is why the method was so practical in 1959: a draughtsman, or a simple machine, could carry it out with a ruler. Bézier's Bernstein sum (see Mathématiques) gives the same point by arithmetic; de Casteljau gives it by geometry. They are two descriptions of one curve.

§ 3 — MathématiquesEn direct, pour le t et le degré réglés à l'atelier

La formule — Bernstein

One sum does all the drawing

B(t) = Σi=0…n  C(n,i) · (1−t)n−i · ti · Pi
Each control point Pi pulls on the curve with a weight. The weights always add up to exactly 1 — so the point B(t) is a weighted average, and it can never leave the convex hull of its points. At t = 0 only P₀ counts; at t = 1, only Pₙ. In between, the pull hands over smoothly. The bars below are those weights, live, for the t you have set.

Why Renault cared: a stylist's clay model could not be sent to a milling machine. Bézier's sum could. Change one number, re-run the sum, and the same panel comes off the machine every time — the beginning of designing cars by computer.

Polynômes de Bernstein

The weights, drawn

Each coloured line is one point's pull as t travels from 0 to 1. The vertical rule is your t. Where a line is high, its point is steering the pen.

Bézier built his system, UNISURF, on these polynomials — work begun at Renault in the early 1960s and presented through the decade. The same Bernstein sum still sits inside every font outline and vector drawing you use today.

Dossier — Deux bureaux d'études, une même courbe

Paris, 1959–1962: the race to draw by numbers

1959

Citroën — secret

Paul de Casteljau, physicist at Citroën, works out the segment-dividing construction. The company classifies it. He may not publish for years.

1962

Renault — publication

Pierre Bézier, engineer at Renault since 1933, publishes his method independently. Because his paper is the first in print, the curves take his name.

1960s

UNISURF

Bézier develops UNISURF at Renault: stylists shape curves and surfaces on a screen, and milling machines cut full-size body masters from the numbers.

Auj.

Partout

The same mathematics drives PostScript and SVG paths, font glyphs, CAD kernels and animation curves. Drag a handle in any drawing program — it is a Bézier handle.
